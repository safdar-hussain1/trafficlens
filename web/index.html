Why there is no speed here

Speed needs a surveyed road plane. None of the three sources on this page has one with an independent along-road anchor, so the engine returns nothing rather than converting pixels into km/h. Stopped-vehicle detection needs the same survey, so it never fires here either — the panel says so instead of sitting empty.

Limits are set by you

There is no speed limit configured on this page. The motorway clip has a limit sign on the median whose digits are not legible at this resolution, so nothing here refers to a posted limit. Where a deployment sets one, it is a user-set number and the interface says which.

What runs where

The model is a YOLO11n graph exported at 480 px, 10.7 MB, downloaded once and kept in Cache Storage. It runs on WebGPU where the machine grants an adapter and on single-threaded WebAssembly where it does not. The badge above names which one you got and what it measured.

Detecting and drawing are separate

The page draws every frame and detects on the cadence the measured backend can actually sustain, then says which it is doing. A machine that cannot detect every frame still tracks and draws smoothly; it just detects less often, and the badge reports that rather than hiding it.

How a crossing is decided

A gate is a directed line segment, not a line. Every frame, each tracked vehicle's anchor — the bottom centre of its box, where it meets the road — moves from one point to the next. If that step's path meets the gate segment, the vehicle is counted once, labelled by the side it ended up on, and never counted again however long it lingers.

The common shortcut is a band: count a vehicle when it is within some pixels of the line. The diagram shows both ways that fails. A fast vehicle steps clean across a thin band between two frames and is never counted; a slow one sits inside the band for a second and is counted on every frame it stays there. Crossing is a property of the path, not of a distance, which is why the engine tests the path — and why the band rule is measured below rather than argued about.

Counting accuracy

One clip, one gate, and a ground-truth set built by hand from a slit-scan of the gate line — the same geometry the diagram above draws. Every crossing is scored one by one against a label rather than by comparing totals, because two totals can agree while every event in them is wrong. The gate rule is measured against the two shortcuts it replaces, and the engine's tracker against two simpler ones.

What happens when the input gets worse

A figure measured on clean footage is a figure measured on the easy case. So the same stream is degraded four ways — decimated, dropped, starved of detections, and jittered — and every method is re-scored at every level. Two of these are exactly the conditions a motion model exists for, which makes them the fair test of whether the engine's tracker earns what it costs.

What identities cost at the gate

Crossing accuracy can hide an identity problem: a split track can still produce one count. This benchmark scores the identities themselves, where they matter — in the region around the gate — with the counting rule held fixed so a difference between two rows belongs to the tracker. It also states plainly which identity claims it is not entitled to make, because this clip has no frame-by-frame identity labels.

Speed, in two tiers

Speed is where a traffic tool is easiest to fake and hardest to check, so it is measured in two separate places. Against a synthetic scene the truth is exact and the answer is a number. Against the real motorway clip the answer is that no absolute speed can honestly be published at all — and that turned out to be one of the strongest results here, because it comes with matched controls.

Tier one: exact truth, and what it excludes

A simulated overpass camera, vehicles driven at known speeds, and the road plane fitted from surveyed correspondences with a holdout. This measures the tracking, homography and speed chain. It does not measure the detector, and it does not measure calibration error — the plane is fitted to the very camera that generated the boxes, so the survey is perfect by construction. That makes these figures a ceiling, not a prediction of field accuracy.

Tier two: the real clip has no scale, and here is the proof

Turning pixels per frame into km/h needs a known distance along the road. The flagship clip was calibrated on an assumed lane-marking period, and measuring the footage falsified the assumption's own justification. A dedicated survey then went looking for an independent along-road anchor — guardrail posts, delineator posts, median barrier joints, any repeating structure, a second marking line — and found none that survives its own controls. So the calibration block was removed from the shipped config, the engine returns nothing for speed on that clip, and no km/h figure derived from it appears anywhere on this page or in the reports behind it.

What this does badly

Nine findings that went against what the project expected. They are here because they are the most useful content on the page: anyone can publish the number that came out well, and a reader has no way to tell which numbers were not published. Each claim is followed by the measurement it rests on.

The engine's own tracker does not pay for itself on this clip

On clean footage its Kalman filter and Hungarian assignment score below both simpler baselines — by one event, so read that one as a tie — and under degradation it scores lowest of the three at every level where they differ. It is also the most expensive of the three by a wide margin. Sophistication that does not earn its cost is a result, not something to bury.

It stops counting entirely at a low frame rate, and mild box jitter costs it real accuracy

At two frames a second it makes no predictions at all — not wrong ones, none. And per-corner jitter of two pixels, which is a small multiple of the box noise measured on this very clip, costs it real accuracy: most of the score survives, and what it loses is many times the one-event resolution below which this page draws no comparison between methods at all. The two figures are below; read them rather than this sentence.

The one degradation it handles gracefully has an undiagnosed cause

Detection dropout is what a motion model is for, and here the engine does degrade smoothly with no cliff. But the association-floor ablation, which explains its other collapses, explicitly does not explain what it loses here. That makes this a second, separate fault, and it is undiagnosed.

Nothing measures the shipped association floor as better than SORT's, except under detection dropout

Loosening the IoU floor recovers a large amount of accuracy the moment the input degrades, and recovers nothing at all on undegraded footage, where the two floors tie exactly. Nothing measured here makes the shipped floor better than the looser one except under detection dropout, where loosening it costs a little — that single result is the only argument these measurements make for keeping it.

Quantising the detector was measured and refused

An int8 dynamic quantisation of the browser model was compared against the float32 one before either shipped. It is a real saving on download and a real loss of detections. In a counting product a missed detection is a missed count, so the trade is refused at any download size and the page pays the full weight.

The band rule fails on crossing time, not crossing count

Its totals look almost right, which is exactly what makes it dangerous: it predicts about as many crossings as there are and lands hardly any of them on the frame the crossing happened. The per-frame rule fails the other way and over-counts wildly — and that over-count is a lower bound, because a vehicle stopped on the gate emits nothing at all where the failure would be worst.

Every accuracy figure here is an upper bound

The evidence rests on one clip and one gate, and that gate was chosen for label reliability rather than for the engine's convenience: its traffic is the largest, best separated and least foreshortened in the frame. That is the easiest case for association as well as for labelling. No second clip was labelled, and no claim is made about one.

The flagship clip cannot be given a speed at all

A scale uncertainty this wide is not a speed figure. Publishing nothing is the defensible product, so the engine returns nothing and the config ships with no calibration block. The survey behind this is above, with its matched controls.

How it is built

One core, written twice. The Python engine is where the measurement happens; the TypeScript engine is what runs in this tab. They are not two implementations trusted separately — every tunable both read is generated from one Python source, and committed fixtures written by the Python engine are replayed through the browser engine in your own browser at ?selftest=1, including cases sitting exactly on each decision boundary.

The published site is a build output that lives in the repository, and a whole-tree manifest records a digest of every file the build reads and every file it writes, so a stale bundle serving next to a green test suite is a test failure rather than something nobody notices. The three fonts are self-hosted as subsetted woff2 and no chart library is fetched from anywhere: the claim that nothing leaves your device after load has to be true of the page as well as of the detector.
